Stay Interviews and Workforce Risk

Stay Interviews for Critical Roles: What CEOs Should Ask Before Talent Leaves

For critical manufacturing and aerospace roles, the exit interview comes too late. Stay interviews give leaders a chance to hear the blockers while there is still time to act.

By Heather MacKay-Mencheski | June 3, 2026 | People Operations for Manufacturing and Aerospace | 3 min read

Executive Answer

A stay interview is not an employee-satisfaction chat. For critical roles, it is a business-continuity tool. Manufacturing and aerospace CEOs should use stay interviews to learn what might cause key people to leave, what blockers are making success harder, and what decisions need executive attention before turnover happens.

Why Exit Interviews Are Too Late

Exit interviews can be useful, but they are a postmortem. By the time a critical engineer, supervisor, technician, inspector, estimator, or maintenance lead is sitting in an exit interview, the business has already lost leverage.

The company may still ask what could have been done differently. The employee may even answer honestly. But the answer often points to something leadership could have heard months earlier.

That is why critical roles need a different rhythm. They need stay interviews before frustration turns into a decision to leave.

What a Stay Interview Should Actually Surface

The goal is not to ask whether people are happy. Happiness is too vague. The better goal is to understand whether the person still believes they can succeed inside the current system.

For manufacturing and aerospace leaders, the best stay interviews surface three things:

  • Blockers: What is making it harder to do the work well?
  • Belief: Does the employee believe leadership will act on what they are seeing?
  • Risk: What would make this person start taking a competitor's call?
The stay interview should help leaders hear the resignation before it becomes one.

The Critical-Role Stay Interview Framework

Three Questions
1. What is making success harder than it needs to be?

This surfaces equipment, staffing, process, decision, communication, and supervisor issues before they become resignation reasons.

2. What have you already raised that has not been addressed?

This reveals whether the company has a listening problem, a decision-rights problem, or a follow-through problem.

3. What would make you believe this is still the right place to do your best work?

This moves the conversation from complaint to retention strategy without pretending every request can be granted.

Who Should Be Interviewed First

Do not start with everyone. Start with roles where the business impact of departure would be high.

For a manufacturer or aerospace supplier, that may include:

  • Engineers tied to product development, customer requirements, or technical problem-solving.
  • Quality leaders who understand regulatory, customer, or inspection history.
  • Maintenance and controls talent who keep equipment running.
  • Supervisors who hold shift consistency and employee trust.
  • Experienced operators, machinists, and technicians whose judgment is not fully documented.

The CEO does not need to personally conduct every interview. But the executive team does need a way to see patterns, assign decisions, and close the loop.

The Follow-Through Rule

A stay interview without follow-through can make retention worse. It teaches people that leadership is willing to ask, but not willing to act.

After every stay interview, the company should document three things:

  1. What did we hear?
  2. What decision or action is required?
  3. What will we tell the employee by what date?

That final step matters. Critical talent does not need leadership to solve everything immediately. They need evidence that leadership is taking the signal seriously.
