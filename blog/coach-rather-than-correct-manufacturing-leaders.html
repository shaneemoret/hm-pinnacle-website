Coach Rather Than Correct: A Habit for Manufacturing Leaders

Coach them in a way they can hear.

John D. Mencheski  |  Published September 16, 2026  |  6 min read

What leaders need to know

Correcting someone in the moment fixes the task and teaches them that being watched is dangerous. Coaching works differently: give people boundaries, be present while they work, then have a specific conversation afterward about what you saw and what to do next time. They keep learning because they know you still have their backing.

Coach them in a way they can hear.

That is the whole idea, and it is the difference between a leader people keep learning from and a leader people learn to avoid.

Correcting in the moment closes people down

When something goes wrong in front of you, the instinct is to fix it immediately and visibly. The work gets corrected. Something else gets damaged.

The person learns that being watched is dangerous. Next time they are unsure, they will not ask, because asking exposes them to the same treatment. You have not removed the mistake. You have removed your visibility into the next one.

I am not arguing for letting things slide. I am arguing that the moment of the error is usually the worst moment to teach, because nobody learns well while they are defending themselves.

Give people boundaries, then watch how they handle the work

Coaching starts before anything goes wrong. Give people boundaries. Be there. Watch how they handle the work.

Boundaries are not restrictions. They are the edges of the decision someone owns, so they know where their judgement applies and where they should come to you. Without them, people either freeze or overreach, and both look like a performance problem when they are actually a clarity problem.

Being there matters as much. If you only appear when something breaks, your presence becomes a signal that something has broken. Leaders who are around routinely get to have ordinary conversations, and ordinary conversations are where coaching actually happens.

The conversation that starts with yesterday

When you see something that can be improved, have a conversation instead of launching an attack. The opening I use is simple:

Yesterday, I saw you in this situation. Let us talk about what we should have done.

Three things are doing work in that sentence. It names a specific moment rather than a general impression, so there is something concrete to discuss. It puts a day between the event and the conversation, so neither of you is running on adrenaline. And it says we, which makes it a shared problem to solve rather than a verdict being delivered.

I had a conversation like that recently. I saw the action change afterward. That is the part of this work that makes it worth doing.

Why the backing is the point

Coaching helps people keep learning and growing because they know you still have their backing. That last part is not a nicety. It is the mechanism.

A person who believes you are still behind them will try the harder thing next week. A person who is not sure will do only what they are certain they can defend. Over a year those two people produce very different work, and the difference has nothing to do with their ability.

This is also how you find out what someone is actually capable of. Stop creating boundaries around what you assume people can do. Give them the opportunity to show you. Let them struggle a little, and be there to coach and help. When they get better, give them something harder.

We all have extraordinary in us. Often it is the leader who is holding it back, without ever intending to.

What usually gets in the way

Most leaders I work with already believe in coaching. They still default to correcting, and there are three reasons that come up repeatedly.

The first is time. Correcting takes thirty seconds and coaching takes fifteen minutes, so under production pressure the thirty-second version wins every time. That maths only works if you ignore how often you end up having the thirty-second conversation again.

The second is that the leader is the most capable person at the task. When you can do it faster and better yourself, taking the work back feels efficient. It is efficient today and expensive over a year, because nobody else ever gets to the point where they can carry it.

The third is discomfort. A correction is impersonal and quick. A coaching conversation means sitting with somebody while they are uncomfortable, and staying in that until something useful comes out of it. That is genuinely harder, and it is most of the job.

What this looks like across a shift

None of this requires a programme or a new meeting. It requires a leader who is present enough to see the work and disciplined enough to wait for the right moment to talk about it.

Walk the floor when nothing is wrong. Ask what someone is working on rather than checking whether it is done. Notice the decisions people make, not only the outcomes. Then pick the one or two worth revisiting tomorrow.

Supervisors who do this build teams that surface problems early. Supervisors who only appear when something breaks build teams that get very good at hiding things until they cannot be hidden.

What separates correcting from coaching in practice

Both involve telling someone something they did not want to hear. The difference shows up in four places:

  • Timing. Correcting happens in the moment and in front of people. Coaching happens afterward and in private.
  • Subject. Correcting is about the error. Coaching is about the decision that produced it.
  • Direction. Correcting looks backward at what happened. Coaching looks forward at what happens next time.
  • Aftermath. Correcting ends the conversation. Coaching starts one that continues.

The test I would apply is whether the person leaves the conversation more willing or less willing to bring you the next problem. That answer tells you which one you actually did.

Challenge them once they are ready

Coaching has a second half that leaders often skip. Once someone proves they can handle what you gave them, give them more.

If you empowered somebody and they did well with it, make the next thing a little tougher. Expand what they own. Put them in a conversation they have not been in before. Hand over a decision you have been making yourself.

People get genuinely excited when they realise they are capable of more than they thought. I have watched it happen often enough to expect it. That excitement is what people mean when they talk about engagement, and it is not something you can generate with an initiative.

The risk of stopping after the first success is that the person concludes the ceiling is where you left it. They stay where you put them, and you conclude that is their limit, when the limit was the assignment.

It is more than correcting one moment

A correction fixes today. Coaching changes what the person does when you are not in the room, which is most of the time.

That is why this is worth the extra effort it takes. You are not trying to get one task done properly. You are trying to build somebody who gets the next fifty done properly without needing you there.

If you want your supervisors doing this consistently, it has to be taught rather than assumed. When coaching is not the right tool

I want to be honest about the limit, because treating coaching as the answer to everything is how leaders end up tolerating things they should not.

Coaching works when someone wants to do the job well and is missing skill, context, or confidence. That covers most situations.

It does not work when someone has been told clearly, understands, is capable, and has decided not to. That is a different conversation, and dressing it up as coaching wastes months while the rest of the team watches nothing happen. Some people also should not be responsible for a team at all, and no amount of coaching changes that.

The distinction is usually visible within two or three conversations. If you have named the specific behaviour, agreed what changes, set a date to review it, and nothing moves, you are no longer coaching. Be straight about which conversation you are actually in.

Where to start this week

Pick one person and one thing you noticed recently that you let pass because the moment was wrong.

Have the conversation tomorrow. Name the specific situation. Ask what they were working with at the time, because there is usually information you did not have. Then agree together on what the approach would be next time.

Watch what happens over the following fortnight. In my experience the change shows up faster than people expect, and the person starts bringing you things earlier, which is the real return.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between coaching and correcting?

Correcting happens in the moment, focuses on the error, looks backward, and ends the conversation. Coaching happens afterward and in private, focuses on the decision behind the error, looks forward to next time, and starts a conversation that continues.

How should a leader open a coaching conversation?

Name a specific moment and invite a joint review: yesterday, I saw you in this situation, let us talk about what we should have done. It gives something concrete to discuss, puts time between the event and the conversation, and makes it a shared problem.

Does coaching mean letting standards slide?

No. The standard does not change. What changes is the timing and the framing, because nobody learns well while they are defending themselves in front of colleagues.

Why does the person needing to know they have your backing matter so much?

Because it determines what they attempt next. Someone who believes you are behind them will try the harder thing. Someone who is unsure does only what they can defend, and over a year those produce very different work.

How do I tell whether I coached or corrected?

Ask whether the person left more willing or less willing to bring you the next problem. That answer tells you which one it actually was.
