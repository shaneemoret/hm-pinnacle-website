Your Best Engineer Should Not Have to Become a Manager to Earn a Promotion

When management is the only way up, companies lose a great technical expert and gain an unprepared manager in the same promotion. A two-path model gives specialists real advancement without forcing them to manage people.

Growing manufacturers work hard to recruit people with specialized skills.

You may spend months looking for the right engineer, controls specialist, CNC programmer, quality expert, maintenance technician, estimator, or process expert. When you finally find that person, they quickly become someone the business depends on.

Then, because they are excellent at the work, the company promotes them into management.

Sometimes that is exactly the right move. Some technical experts want to lead people, and with the right preparation, they become outstanding managers.

But sometimes it is the only move available.

The employee has reached the top of the technical ladder. If they want more pay, recognition, influence, or opportunity, the organization tells them, directly or indirectly, that they must begin managing people.

That creates a problem for the employee and the business.

The company can lose a great technical expert and gain an unprepared manager in the same promotion.

For CEOs growing manufacturing operations, management should not be the only path upward. A stronger model gives high-performing employees two credible ways to advance:

What to Review
  1. A people-leadership path for employees who want to build teams, coach performance, make staffing decisions, and lead through others.
  2. A technical-expert path for employees who want to deepen their craft, solve harder problems, transfer knowledge, improve systems, and influence the business without taking on direct reports.

Both paths should offer meaningful progression. Both should carry status. Both should have clear expectations. And both should be connected to what the business needs as it grows.

Why the One-Path Promotion Model Breaks Down

Technical excellence and people leadership are not the same capability.

A strong engineer may be exceptional at diagnosing failures, improving processes, protecting quality, or translating customer requirements into reliable production. A strong manager must set expectations, give feedback, handle conflict, develop employees, make fair decisions, communicate across functions, and hold people accountable.

There can be overlap, but one skill set does not automatically produce the other.

When a company promotes someone solely because they are the strongest technical performer, it may place that person in a role they did not want and were not prepared for. The new manager may still be expected to carry much of the technical workload while also handling schedules, performance issues, meetings, coaching, and employee concerns.

Now the organization has created two risks.

First, critical technical work may suffer because the expert has less time to do it.

Second, employees may be led by someone who has never been taught how to lead.

This is not a character flaw in the person who was promoted. It is a design flaw in the organization.

The business offered one definition of success: move away from the work you are great at and start managing people.

The Hidden Recruiting and Retention Cost

Manufacturers often describe specialized talent as difficult to find, but recruiting is only one part of the problem. The other part is whether talented people can see a future after they arrive.

An experienced engineer or technical specialist is paying attention to more than the current job description. They are also asking:

What to Review
  • What can I become here?
  • Can I keep growing without leaving the work I value?
  • Will deeper expertise be recognized, or will I eventually hit a ceiling?
  • Does this company understand the difference between technical contribution and people management?

If the only visible next step is supervisor or manager, employees who do not want that role have three choices: stay in place, accept a poor-fit promotion, or leave for an organization that values senior technical contribution.

That means a company can lose specialized talent even when compensation is competitive and the employee likes the work.

The issue is not always a lack of opportunity. Sometimes it is a narrow definition of opportunity.

What a Two-Path Promotion Model Looks Like

A two-path model creates parallel career ladders instead of treating management as the universal destination.

The exact titles will vary by business, but the structure might look something like this:

Technical path

Engineer → Senior Engineer → Lead or Principal Engineer → Technical Fellow or Enterprise Expert

People-leadership path

Engineer → Team Lead → Engineering Manager → Director or Functional Leader

This approach also applies beyond engineering. A manufacturer might create expert progression for quality, maintenance, tooling, automation, machining, scheduling, estimating, safety, or other roles where deep knowledge protects output and growth.

The titles matter less than the credibility of the system.

Employees will quickly see through a technical path that exists only on paper. If the management path receives more pay, more access to executives, more recognition, and more decision-making authority, the company is still sending the same message: management is the real promotion.

A genuine two-path model makes both routes valuable.

The Technical Path Is Not a Consolation Prize

A senior technical role should not be a place to park someone who does not want to manage. It should have a clear business purpose.

Technical leaders can create value by:

What to Review
  • Solving the highest-risk or most complex problems.
  • Improving processes, quality, reliability, and technical standards.
  • Leading major technical projects without becoming the manager of everyone involved.
  • Mentoring less-experienced employees and strengthening the skills pipeline.
  • Capturing knowledge that currently lives in one person's head.
  • Supporting customer, supplier, engineering, quality, and operations decisions.
  • Evaluating new equipment, automation, or technical methods.
  • Reducing the company's dependence on a small number of irreplaceable people.

These employees may lead work, decisions, standards, and projects without being responsible for performance reviews, attendance issues, compensation conversations, or daily people management.

That distinction is important.

Leadership is broader than management. A person can have enormous influence without having direct reports.

The Management Path Must Be Chosen and Built

Creating a technical path does not make people managers less important. It makes the management path more intentional.

Before promoting a technical employee into management, the company should determine whether the person understands and wants the actual role.

Do they want to coach others, including employees who learn differently than they do?

Are they willing to have difficult conversations?

Can they set clear expectations instead of simply fixing the work themselves?

Can they move from being personally responsible for the answer to being responsible for building a team that can find the answer?

Do they understand that their success will increasingly be measured through other people's performance?

Interest alone is not enough, but it is a necessary starting point.

From there, the organization needs to build leadership capability before and after the promotion. That includes training, coaching, clear decision rights, consistent performance standards, and support from the manager above them.

Promoting someone and then hoping they figure it out is not leadership development.

How to Build Two Credible Paths

The model does not need to begin as a large HR initiative. It can start with the roles creating the greatest growth, recruiting, and continuity risk.

1. Identify the specialized roles the business cannot afford to lose

Look beyond titles. Ask where the organization relies on scarce expertise, customer-specific knowledge, certifications, process history, technical judgment, or relationships that would be difficult to replace.

These are the roles where a technical career path may have the greatest value.

2. Define what greater contribution looks like without direct reports

For each technical level, describe how the employee's impact expands.

That progression might include greater problem complexity, broader influence across departments or sites, ownership of standards, mentoring responsibilities, major-project leadership, knowledge transfer, technical risk reduction, or input into strategic investments.

The path should reward increased contribution, not simply years of service.

3. Define the people-leadership path separately

Spell out what changes when an employee becomes responsible for people.

The role should include expectations for communication, coaching, accountability, employee development, workforce planning, conflict management, and cross-functional leadership. Technical credibility may still matter, but it should no longer be the only measure of success.

4. Make pay and recognition comparable

The two tracks do not need identical compensation at every level, but a senior expert should not have to become a manager merely to reach the next meaningful pay band.

Review access and recognition too. Are technical experts invited into the decisions where their knowledge matters? Are they visible to senior leadership? Are their contributions recognized with the same seriousness as a manager's?

If not, the technical path will not feel real.

5. Let employees test leadership before committing to it

Use project leadership, mentoring, onboarding support, temporary team-lead assignments, or improvement initiatives to help employees experience parts of leadership before taking a permanent management role.

This gives the employee and the company better information. It also makes it easier to discover that someone prefers technical leadership without treating that preference as a failure.

6. Allow movement between paths

Career decisions do not have to be permanent.

An employee may spend several years deepening technical expertise and later decide to manage. A new manager may discover that their best contribution is on the technical path. If the organization treats that move as a thoughtful realignment instead of a demotion, it can retain the person and place them where they create the most value.

Questions CEOs Should Ask

If your company is growing and specialized roles are becoming harder to recruit, ask:

What to Review
  • Which employees have reached the top of their current role but still have room to grow?
  • Who has been promoted into management primarily because they were technically strong?
  • Which managers are still carrying so much technical work that they cannot lead their teams well?
  • Where would losing one expert create quality, delivery, customer, or continuity risk?
  • Can a technical employee gain pay, status, and influence without taking direct reports?
  • Do employees understand the difference between leading work and managing people?
  • Are future managers being developed before the promotion, or trained only after problems appear?

The answers will show whether your career structure supports growth or quietly works against it.

Build Careers That Protect the Business

Growing manufacturers need strong managers. They also need deep technical expertise.

Those needs should not compete with each other.

When management is the only path upward, the business pressures skilled employees into roles that may not fit. It weakens the technical bench, creates avoidable leadership gaps, and makes it harder for specialized talent to imagine a long-term future with the company.

A two-path promotion model gives employees a more honest choice.

Some will choose to lead people. Build them for it.

Others will choose to lead through expertise, judgment, mentorship, and technical influence. Build a real future for them too.

The goal is not to keep great people in the same jobs forever. The goal is to stop confusing advancement with management.

When employees can grow in the direction of their strengths, the company retains more capability, develops better leaders, and builds a workforce that can support the next stage of growth.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a dual career ladder?

A dual career ladder gives employees two credible ways to advance: a people-leadership path for those who want to lead teams, and a technical-expert path for those who want to deepen their craft and influence the business without direct reports. Both carry real progression, status, and pay.

Why do manufacturers lose engineers after promoting them?

Technical excellence and people leadership are different capabilities. When the only promotion available is management, a strong engineer may accept a role they did not want and are not prepared for. The business loses the technical output and gains a struggling manager.

How do you start building a technical-expert path?

Define levels with real criteria, attach meaningful pay progression, name the influence each level carries, and make the path visible during hiring and performance conversations so employees know it exists before they start looking elsewhere.
