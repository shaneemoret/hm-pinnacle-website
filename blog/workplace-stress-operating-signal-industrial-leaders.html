Workplace Stress Is an Operating Signal, Not Just a Wellness Topic

Workplace stress can expose unclear priorities, excessive overtime, weak handoffs, and inconsistent leadership before performance suffers.

Heather MacKay-Mencheski  |  Published July 22, 2026  |  6 min read

What leaders need to know

The warning rarely arrives as one dramatic event. It appears in the same experienced employees carrying overtime again. A supervisor answering every question because the handoff is unclear. A new hire waiting for training while the production schedule keeps moving. An employee raising the same blocker for the third week and receiving no answer.

The warning rarely arrives as one dramatic event.

It appears in the same experienced employees carrying overtime again. A supervisor answering every question because the handoff is unclear. A new hire waiting for training while the production schedule keeps moving. An employee raising the same blocker for the third week and receiving no answer.

Each issue can look isolated. Together, they tell leaders something important about how the work is designed and led.

Stress is not always a personal resilience problem. In an operationally intensive business, it can be the earliest visible signal that priorities are unclear, staffing is fragile, overtime is concentrated, supervisors are inconsistent, or employees do not trust the escalation path.

If leaders reduce the issue to self-care, they may miss the operating problem underneath it.

Wellness support cannot repair broken work

A wellness benefit can be valuable. It can give employees access to real support.

But it cannot repair a schedule that changes without notice. It cannot resolve two managers giving conflicting direction. It cannot create backup capacity for the one experienced employee everyone depends on. It cannot make an overloaded supervisor follow through.

The first red flag is a gap between what the company offers and what the work requires.

NIOSH's Total Worker Health approach starts at the organizational and environmental level. Its hierarchy prioritizes eliminating or reducing conditions that contribute to poor worker health and well-being before relying on individual-level interventions.

That does not make personal support unimportant. It means leaders should not ask employees to cope with operating conditions the company has the authority to improve.

The signal is often visible before someone leaves

Leaders often learn about the pressure too late.

The employee resigns. The exit interview mentions workload, communication, schedule instability, or a supervisor problem. Only then does the organization connect the dots.

By that point, the stress had probably been visible elsewhere:

  • Overtime concentrated among the same reliable people.
  • Absences or tardiness increasing on one shift.
  • New hires taking longer to become independent.
  • Experienced employees becoming impatient or withdrawn.
  • Supervisors escalating more conflict and fewer solutions.
  • Quality or safety concerns appearing during periods of heavy coverage.
  • Employees raising the same blocker without seeing a response.

Those are not perfect diagnostic measures. They are signals that deserve a leadership conversation before the exit interview.

Read stress as a signal of where the system is absorbing pressure

Every growing company creates pressure. Growth adds customers, shifts, managers, deadlines, and handoffs. The goal is not to remove every demanding moment.

The goal is to know where normal pressure has become chronic operating strain.

NIOSH defines job stress as the harmful physical and emotional response that can occur when job requirements do not match a worker's capabilities, resources, or needs. That definition points leaders toward the design of the work: requirements, resources, control, clarity, and support.

For a CEO or COO, the practical question is:

What is the workforce having to absorb because the system has not made a decision?

Sometimes the answer is headcount. Sometimes it is poor prioritization. Sometimes it is a supervisor who has never been taught to lead. Sometimes it is a process owned by three functions and completed by none.

Leaders still own the conditions they can control

An employee assistance program, coaching resource, or wellness benefit creates access to support. That access matters.

It does not transfer ownership of workplace conditions to the employee.

Executives own priorities and tradeoffs. Operations owns workload and production reality. Supervisors own daily clarity, communication, and escalation. HR owns the infrastructure that helps leaders see patterns, act consistently, and connect employees with support.

A healthier workplace emerges when those owners make better decisions together.

A workplace-stress operating check

Bring HR and operations into the same room and ask:

  • Where is overtime concentrated by team, shift, and role?
  • Which employees are functioning as permanent backup coverage?
  • Where do schedules change with the least notice?
  • Which teams have the highest absence, conflict, or early-turnover signals?
  • Where do employees receive conflicting priorities?
  • Which supervisors have the widest spans or the most new hires?
  • What concerns have been raised more than once without a visible response?
  • Which decisions are being carried by employees because leaders have not resolved the ownership?

Do not use the answers to label a team as stressed. Use them to find where the system may be creating avoidable strain.

A practical four-part response

1. Remove one repeat source of friction

Choose a problem employees encounter every week: late schedule changes, unclear handoffs, duplicate approvals, missing equipment, or contradictory direction. Fix the operating cause, then tell the team what changed.

2. Give supervisors a usable leadership routine

Supervisors need more than encouragement to check in. Give them a short weekly structure: priorities, workload risk, employee concerns, recognition, and unresolved follow-up.

3. Connect people signals to production signals

Review overtime, absences, early turnover, training delays, safety, quality, and supervisor escalation together. The pattern is often more useful than any single measure.

4. Close the response loop

Employees do not need every request approved. They do need to know that raising a problem leads to a decision, an explanation, or a next step.

Silence turns normal pressure into distrust.

What leaders should do this week

Pick one team where the pressure is visible. Do not launch a survey first.

Ask the supervisor and two or three employees the same question: What part of the work is harder than it needs to be right now?

Compare the answers with overtime, absence, training, safety, and quality signals. Choose one fix the business can own. Assign it, set a date, and report back.

That is not a full wellness strategy. It is a credible first act of leadership.

The closing point

Workplace stress is personal in its impact, but it is often operational in its origin.

Leaders who treat it only as a wellness topic will keep funding support around the edges. Leaders who read it as an operating signal can improve the conditions creating the pressure in the first place.

FAQ

Is all workplace stress caused by the employer?

No. People carry personal and work-related pressures, and not every source is within an employer's control. The leadership responsibility is to identify and improve the work conditions, resources, clarity, and support the organization can control.

What workplace signals should leaders review?

Useful signals include overtime concentration, absenteeism patterns, early turnover, training delays, supervisor span, unresolved escalations, safety and quality patterns, and recurring themes from employee conversations.

Are wellness benefits still worthwhile?

They can be. Benefits and employee support create valuable access. They work best alongside leadership action on workload, clarity, scheduling, supervisor capability, and other organizational conditions.
