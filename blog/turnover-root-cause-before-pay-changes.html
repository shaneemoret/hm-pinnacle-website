Diagnose Turnover Before Changing Compensation

What leaders need to know

Illustrative operating example — not a client result

A plant assumes technicians are leaving for better pay

Imagine an industrial manufacturer losing experienced technicians from one production cell. Leaders hear that a competitor is hiring and propose an immediate pay increase. Exit conversations and stay interviews reveal a more complicated picture: weekend overtime is unpredictable, one senior technician carries most training, and advancement criteria are unclear. Pay still needs to be checked against the market.

The leadership team separates those issues. It tests whether compensation is competitive, assigns an operations owner to stabilize the schedule, and gives HR and the cell supervisor a plan for training coverage and career expectations. A later review asks whether those specific conditions improved, instead of assuming a single raise solved turnover.

Separate a theory from evidence

A competitor’s offer may be visible while the employee’s earlier frustrations were not. Ask what departing employees actually said and whether current employees describe the same issue.

Listen across more than one source

Use exit interviews, stay conversations, manager observations, and patterns by team or role. Look for workload, development, supervision, schedules, and pay. One anecdote should start an investigation, not end it.

Decide what to fix first

If pay is clearly out of line, address it. If people describe inconsistent leadership or stalled growth, a raise alone may not repair the experience. Name the owner of each proposed change and how you will know whether it helped.

Close the loop with employees

Tell people what the organization learned and which changes it can make. Explain what cannot change yet. Silence after asking for feedback makes the next conversation less candid.

A turnover diagnosis before a pay change

Segment departures by role, shift, supervisor, tenure, and reason before deciding that compensation is the root cause. Compare the pattern with recruiting feedback, stay conversations, scheduling changes, and promotion opportunities.

Ask whether employees had the tools, training, and clear expectations needed to succeed. In an aerospace or manufacturing environment, a recurring departure from one production cell may point to a supervisor or handoff problem that a uniform raise cannot solve.

If pay remains a credible factor, review it with the full evidence and applicable compensation requirements. Choose one intervention, name an owner, and check whether the affected group actually stays longer.

Frequently asked questions

Should a company ignore compensation in a turnover review?

No. Compensation is one possible cause and should be checked with the same care as the other likely causes.

What is the first useful question?

Ask what evidence you have about why people left or considered leaving, then compare it with what current employees say.

How do leaders know a fix worked?

Track the specific problem they intended to solve and revisit employee feedback and retention patterns over time.
